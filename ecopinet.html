EcoPinet

Packaging with a purpose. Paper, bags and boxes made from pineapple waste that would otherwise be burned, with seeds inside so they grow into something after use.

01

The problem

After harvest, pineapple farms are left with leaves and stems, and the cheapest way to clear them is to burn them. Open burning of crop waste is one of the biggest sources of PM2.5, the fine dust that gets deep into the lungs and bloodstream.

Pineapple is one of the most wasteful crops: about 55% of the plant is thrown away, around 2.7 million tons a year where we started. At the same time, packaging is the single biggest use of plastic.

Two problems, one material. What if the waste being burned could replace the plastic being thrown away?

02

From farm to prototype

We visited paper and packaging factories to learn the process, then went to three pineapple farms, talked to the farmers, and collected over 260 kg of leaves and stems.

Back home we experimented: shredding the fibers, mixing them with recycled paper, and testing binders. Most were either toxic or didn't hold. Okra mucilage, the sticky gel inside okra, held well, was plant-based and non-toxic, and was cheap to make in bulk.

03

Key decisions

Seeds in the paper: every product has vegetable seeds pressed into it. Tear it up, plant it, water it, and it breaks down in 4–6 weeks into fertilizer and new plants.

Strong enough to replace plastic: our paper reached up to 84.1 MPa tensile strength and held up better against weight and moisture than normal paper.

Start with hotels and cafés: hospitality uses huge amounts of single-use plastic and needs to show sustainability, so we designed amenity kits, coffee sleeves and coasters for them first.

Made with farmers: the process is simple enough to teach, so farmers can make the paper themselves and earn from waste they used to burn.

04

What we made

A full product line from one material: paper bags in three sizes, boxes, tissue boxes, wrapping paper and packaging cushion, plus hotel amenity kits and coffee sleeves for cafés.

We also designed a mock-up app for farmers to schedule waste pickups and see their impact.

05

Outcome

We pitched EcoPinet on GMM25 TV to a panel of experts and were invited to speak at FarmExpo to more than 500 people. We brought samples to hotels and tested the product with real audiences.

Our model: paper at about $0.06 to make and $0.10 to sell, boxes at $0.23 and up to $0.50, with break-even at 12,440 units. We planned to give 70% of net profit to schools in the farming communities.

06

Reflection

EcoPinet was the first time I took an idea from a kitchen experiment to a TV studio. The hardest part wasn't the paper. It was showing that a material can carry a story: farmers who stop burning, a hotel that can show its guests what it's doing, and packaging that grows something after it's used.

It's also where I learned I love the space between design and business, and that's what brought me to study it.
