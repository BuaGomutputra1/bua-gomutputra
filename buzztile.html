BUZZTILE · EXTENDED PROJECT QUALIFICATION (EPQ) · SOLO PROJECT

BuzzTile

Can we walk our way into a greener future? A honeycomb floor tile that turns footsteps into renewable energy, and an app that turns that energy into a game.

ROLE
Everything: research, circuit, code, CAD, fabrication, app
TIMELINE
Nov 2024 – Nov 2025
TYPE
Extended Project Qualification (EPQ)
SKILLS
Circuit design · ESP32 & C++ · Fusion 360 · CNC & laser cutting · HTML, CSS, JS · Gamification

WHAT I MADE

  • A 1:7 scale floor mat of seven hexagon tiles with piezoelectric sensors inside
  • An ESP32 circuit that counts every step and shows it live on an LCD
  • A web app where your steps grow a beehive, unlock bees, and add up for the whole school

02 · THE PROBLEM

Sustainability feels invisible

Everyone learns about climate change in school, but almost no one has ever seen a greenhouse gas or felt their carbon footprint. The causes stay invisible, and the problem feels so big that it’s hard to believe one person can make a difference.

At my school, sustainability was a few pages in a textbook. Being told to recycle and turn off the lights wasn’t going to spark real change. So I asked: what if a footstep, something everyone does all day, could make energy you can actually see?

MY DESIGN BRIEF

  1. 1Make energy from footstepsConvert steps into measurable electrical energy.
  2. 2Show it in real timeDetect each step and display the data instantly.
  3. 3Easy and fun to useAn engaging, intuitive interface for ages 6–18.
  4. 4Spark curiosityGet people asking questions about sustainability.

04 · IDEATION

Three rounds to a honeycomb

Lots of trial and error. The honeycomb won because it can keep growing, and because one bee can’t do much, but a hive can.

05 · BUILDING IT

Three weeks of a blank screen

The goal was simple: piezo sensors make voltage when stepped on, a microcontroller catches the spikes, and an LCD shows them live. The first time I switched it on, the screen stayed blank and nothing was detected. Here’s what it took to fix it:

  1. Voltage almost undetectableThe sensors were wired in series. I rewired them in parallel.
  2. Readings kept cutting outTwisted wires weren’t enough. I soldered every joint.
  3. Screen still blankI’d assumed two 100 Ω resistors equal one 10 kΩ. They don’t. I swapped in the right one.
  4. Too many wires to trustI replaced the 16-pin LCD with an I2C module: four wires instead of twelve.
  5. Two boards doing one jobI dropped the Arduino and let the ESP32 handle everything, from reading steps to Wi-Fi.

Once it worked, I modelled the tiles in Shapr3D, refined them in Fusion 360, and built a 1:7 scale model to save material and cost.

Then two weeks at a local makerspace: cutting, soldering and sanding.

09 · TAKEAWAYS

What I’m taking with me

  1. Most of the work is the part nobody sees. The final tile looks clean, but there were so many things that went wrong, and times I really felt like giving up. It taught me independence.
  2. Know your limits, then design around them. Piezo sensors aren’t made for constant heavy impact. Mine started at 3–5 volts a step and dropped noticeably in just three months.
  3. Next: full size, real hallways. Tougher materials or a hybrid system, then a full-scale test in a school corridor. Done properly, I can see it in schools, parks and airports.

This project changed how I see design: it isn’t just about solving problems, but about reimagining systems, connecting people, and making invisible things like energy and climate visible and human.
